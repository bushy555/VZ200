DROPBEAR 3D

The outback bites back, at sixteen frames a second, on a 1985 home computer.
Drop bears have overrun the station. Armed with a blaster and a jar of Vegemite, you head out past the homestead, through the bush and a gum tree maze, down an old mine, across a misty billabong and up the ridge to Yowie's Lair. Nine connected areas, ten kinds of creature and three bosses stand between you and your ute.
DROPBEAR 3D is a full first-person shooter for the Dick Smith VZ300, with textured walls, creatures that explode into bouncing chunks, furniture to dodge, keys, switches, secret walls, an automap and a three-channel title tune from a one-bit speaker. It's all Z80 machine code, running on a 3.5 MHz CPU with a 128x64 screen.
It is forgiving by design. There's no instant death: if you go down, you start again at the door you came in by, with full health and everything you'd collected.
Vibed by BPM, 2026.
FEATURES
Nine connected areas in six themes: station, bush, hedge maze, mine, swamp, ridge and lair. Each has its own textures and colour scheme, and each area remembers what you've beaten and opened.
Ten creatures, each with its own behaviour:
Drop bears.
Fruit bats and magpies that swoop at eye level.
Cane toads that hop in bursts.
Slow, hard-biting snakes.
Quick huntsman spiders and dingoes.
Three bosses:
Big Fella.
The Bunyip, which lunges in bursts.
The Yowie, who gets faster as it gets hurt.
Satisfying deaths: beaten creatures burst into chunky pieces that fly, fall and bounce off the floor. Bosses go up with a screen flash.
Furniture:
Oil drums, mine carts, shrubs, termite mounds and stumps block your way.
Lamps, reeds and bones just set the scene.
Puzzles:
Keys open padlocked doors, and lever switches open barred gates.
Cracked walls hide secrets, with 20 opals to find.
A map scroll unlocks the automap in the mazes.
Arcade attract loop:
A dripping, doom-metal title logo and a tense three-channel beeper tune.
A self-deprecating gallery of the locals, each with its own fanfare.
An AI demo that plays a different area each time round.
TECH SPECS
Machine
Dick Smith VZ300: Z80A at 3.54 MHz, BennVenn SD loader with 128K RAM expansion
Display
Mode 1: 128x64 pixels, 4 colours, with both colour sets used (green/yellow/blue/red and buff/cyan/magenta/orange)
Engine
32-column textured raycaster with DDA ray stepping, log-domain distance-to-height tables, and compiled wall scalers for 49 wall heights
Frame rate
About 16 frames a second (median), dipping to about 14 in the busiest rooms
Sprites
Depth-buffered, scaled and mirrored, drawn by unrolled code; frames are stored across two RAM banks
Objects
32 per area: creatures, pickups and furniture. View-cone culling keeps off-screen objects almost free
Particles
32 chunky 2x2 pieces with gravity and floor bounce
Sound
Three-channel interleaved-pulse music engine running at about 26.9 kHz on the one-bit speaker, plus in-game sound effects
Content
9 areas, 6 themes, 10 creature types (including 3 bosses), 5 pickup types, 8 furniture types, 20 opals
Controls
Keyboard (W/S walk, A/D turn, Q/E strafe, SPACE fire, M map) or joystick
File
DROPBEAR.VZ, 26,593 bytes, self-unpacking. It loads at $8000–$E7C8 and checks for both RAM banks before it starts
Memory
Uses nearly all of the expansion. See the memory map below
Toolchain
A custom Z80 assembler and cycle-counting simulator written in Python. A bot played the whole game through in the simulator, checking every area, boss and opal; engine timings were checked against a real VZ300

RUNNING IT
Copy release/DROPBEAR.VZ to the BennVenn SD card and run it. Leave it alone on the title to watch the attract loop, or press fire to play. The game needs the 128K expansion; without it, the game says so instead of crashing.
